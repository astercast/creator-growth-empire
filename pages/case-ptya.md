# The Cohort Course: Ali Abdaal's Part-Time YouTuber Academy

> Ali Abdaal turned a YouTube channel into a course business where, in his own words, "if 400 people join a cohort, I can make a million dollars in just a few days." The course made more money than everything else in his business combined.

## The arc: 0 → scale

Abdaal started his YouTube channel in 2017 while studying medicine at Cambridge, a phone, a bed, a vlog. He reinvested 100% of his early YouTube income into an editor, posted 100 videos to reach 58,000 subscribers, and taught productivity and studying, things he was genuinely expert in.

During the pandemic he launched the Part-Time YouTuber Academy (PTYA), a live cohort course teaching professionals to build YouTube channels as a side project. The first cohort in November 2020 drew about 400 people and generated roughly £220,000. The course then compounded: his maker business generated over $1.3 million in revenue in 2021 (Digiday), with the course "significantly more money than anything" else he ran. A later final cohort reportedly generated $2 million. And in a 2026 Kit case study, Abdaal reports $5 million in revenue from the combined Kit email engine + YouTube funnel, including $528,000 in four days from a single automated Black Friday email sequence.

## The revenue stack

| Stream | Role in the stack | What was reported |
|---|---|---|
| PTYA cohort course ($1,000–$4,000 price points) | Primary income line | $1.3M+ maker revenue (2021); $2M final cohort (reported); $5M combined with Kit email engine |
| Skillshare classes | Early scale + discovery | ~£350,000 in 2020 alone |
| YouTube ads + sponsorships | Top of funnel | ~£136,000 in sponsorships (2020) |
| Email list (Kit) | The actual business asset | $528K in 4 days from one automated sequence (Black Friday) |

The stack's real structure: free YouTube content → email list → cohort launches. Abdaal's own hierarchy: he'd rather have a 500,000-person email list than 5 million YouTube subscribers.

## The 3 decisions that mattered

1. **He taught what he was two steps ahead in.** Studying, productivity, then YouTube itself, each course topic was something he had just figured out, with receipts. Students pay for the shortcut through a path someone credibly walked. Expertise you can prove beats expertise you claim.

2. **He chose the cohort model, not the evergreen course.** Live lectures, Q&As, community, deadlines, and premium pricing ($1,000–$4,000) justified by accountability and access. High prices meant students had skin in the game, which drove the completion and success stories that sold the next cohort. The format WAS the product strategy.

3. **He made the email list the business.** The $528K-in-4-days Black Friday sequence, run while his team was on vacation, is the whole lesson in one anecdote. Social platforms are discovery; the list is destiny. Every subscriber is an asset he can contact on his own schedule, with no brand, algorithm, or deadline in between.

## What to steal

- **Sell the transformation you just achieved.** The course came from his own recent climb (doctor → creator). Teach the last two years of your life, not the last twenty, proximity is credibility.
- **Cohorts beat catalogs.** Live accountability commands premium prices and produces the success stories that market the next launch. Deadlines are a feature, not a bug.
- **Own the list before you need it.** The email engine printed $528K while nobody was working. Build the list when you don't need the money, so it's there when you do.
- **Reinvest early revenue into capacity.** 100% of his first YouTube income went to an editor. Capacity buys consistency; consistency buys the audience that buys the course.

## What NOT to copy

- **He launched to a multi-year audience.** Years of free videos, a Cambridge medicine background, and hundreds of thousands of viewers preceded the first cohort. A $2,000 course with no audience is a product with no customers, the funnel came first.
- **Cohort-course economics are harder now.** 2020–2021 was peak cohort-course appetite (pandemic + creator-education boom). The format still works, but the market is crowded and price-sensitive. The pricing power he had was partly era-specific.
- **High-ticket needs high trust.** $1,000–$4,000 price tags work when the teacher's results are public and documented. Without visible receipts, premium pricing reads as presumption.
- **"A million dollars in a few days" is launch math, not recurring math.** Cohort revenue comes in bursts between launches; the business between them is content, list-building, and iteration. Don't budget the spike as the baseline.
