The Budget-Grocery Newsletter

A budget-grocery creator turned one viral 2023 grocery video into 3M+ followers, then made a paid newsletter — not ads, not sponsors — the primary income line of a seven-figure business run by two full-time employees.

3 min read/684 words

The arc: 0 → scale

She posted budget-grocery content for a long stretch without much traction. The format was simple: what to buy, what it costs, what it becomes. In 2023 one grocery-list video crossed a million views fast, and she has said she was genuinely shocked — nothing like it had happened before. The audience arrived all at once: over 3 million followers across platforms, per the interview.

What happened next is the case: instead of riding rented reach, she stacked owned and diversified lines one at a time. Brand partnerships came first. Then a cookbook. Then affiliate income. Then her own website. Then a paid newsletter — which she calls "the primary primary thing" in the business. Today it is a seven-figure operation with two full-time employees and several contractors.

The newsletter figure is the one that matters and the one most people misread: $670K a year from a newsletter does not happen without the audience, the trust, and the niche. Grocery and meal-planning buyers are among the highest-intent audiences on the internet — advertisers and subscribers both pay for access to a kitchen that is actively being planned.

The 3 decisions that mattered

  1. She built a repeatable series, not a viral moment. "Budget grocery lists" is a format that never runs out: prices change, weeks change, families change. Every video is a new episode of the same promise. One viral video is luck; a format that absorbs luck is a system.
  1. She made audience ownership the primary line. Sponsors and affiliates pay rent; a paid newsletter is a mortgage she collects. The transition to paid subscriptions also created a more direct relationship with readers — the community she owns, not the feed that owns her.
  1. She professionalized early. A content calendar planned months ahead. Two full-time employees plus contractors. Strategic relationships with other creators, deliberately and professionally. Most creators hire at the burnout point; she hired at the growth point, which is why the growth survived.

What to steal

  • Pick a format with infinite episodes. "What I buy + what it costs + what I cook" has a thousand installments. If your concept can't survive 100 episodes, it's a gimmick, not a business.
  • Convert attention into ownership. Every viral spike should end somewhere you control: an email list, a paid tier, a community. Rented reach decays; owned lists compound.
  • Plan months ahead. A structured content calendar is what separates a creator business from a creator hobby. Her edge was operational, not just creative.
  • Hire before the breakdown. Two full-time people and contractors let her keep shipping while the newsletter scaled. Margin spent on capacity buys the next revenue line.

What NOT to copy

  • Don't expect $670K/yr newsletter math from a cold start. That line sits on top of 3M+ followers and years of trust in a niche with extreme purchase intent. Newsletters convert a small fraction of readers to paid — the math only works at real scale.
  • Don't copy the category blindly. Grocery/meal-planning is advertiser-friendly and affiliate-friendly in a way most niches aren't. Your niche's purchase intent sets your ceiling.
  • The figures are self-reported. Every number here comes from the creator's own interview, not audited financials. Treat the structure as proven and the figures as directional.
