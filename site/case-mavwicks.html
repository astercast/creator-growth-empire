Case File

Mavwicks on TikTok Shop

Megan Reep's fragrance brand did roughly $300,000–$400,000 in annual sales before TikTok Shop. In its first year on the platform, it did $32 million.

4 min read/791 words

The arc: 0 → scale

Mavwicks Fragrances was a small fragrance business doing low-six-figure annual sales through conventional channels. Then it joined TikTok Shop — and, per founder Megan Reep in a Modern Retail interview, jumped to $32 million in sales in its first year on the platform.

The mechanism was not a better website. It was founder-led video and live selling, treated as entertainment. Mavwicks runs livestreams like events — one session featured a dunk tank where every bundle purchase dumped her husband into the tank — because "keeping exciting things happening on screen" keeps people watching, and watching people buy. She has credited the platform with taking her small business "to the moon."

The backdrop matters: TikTok Shop shared with Modern Retail that U.S. small businesses on the platform increased sales 66% in 2025, with 215,000+ small businesses actively selling. Mavwicks is the extreme end of a real, platform-wide pattern — not a lone miracle.

The revenue stack

StreamRole in the stackWhat was reported

Note what's absent from the reporting: affiliate-driven discovery and any pre-existing retail or DTC lines. This was a creator-commerce business where the shop IS the business.

The 3 decisions that mattered

  1. The founder is the talent. Smaller brands win on TikTok Shop when founders appear in their own videos and speak directly to customers. Reep didn't hire a spokesperson; she became one. Authenticity is the algorithm's native language, and a founder telling her own story out-converts polished creative.
  1. Livestreams as entertainment events, not infomercials. A dunk tank isn't a sales tactic — it's a show that happens to sell. The session stays alive because something is always happening, and purchases become part of the entertainment. This is the QVC-to-TikTok leap: commerce as content.
  1. She moved onto the rail early and committed. Mavwicks didn't dabble — it went all-in on a channel where U.S. GMV was growing explosively. New distribution rails reward the early: lower competition, subsidized traffic, platform promotion. Being there in force during the growth window mattered as much as the tactics.

What to steal

  • Founder-on-camera beats hired creative. If you sell something, you are the best ad. Talk to the camera like a person, not a brand.
  • Make the live a show. Give viewers a reason to stay beyond the product: games, stakes, guests, spectacle. Every extra minute of watch time is extra chances to convert.
  • Bundle for the impulse. Bundle purchases drove the dunk-tank moment — packaging products into deal bundles raises average order value and gives the live a call-to-action with urgency.
  • Treat new rails as land grabs. When a platform is growing 60%+ a year, presence compounds. The worst time to start was yesterday; the second-worst time is after the subsidies end.

What NOT to copy

  • $32M in sales is not $32M in profit. TikTok Shop's subsidy era meant heavy discounting, free-shipping expectations, and affiliate commissions that eat margin. Gross merchandise value is a revenue number, not a business number — the profit story is undisclosed.
  • Fragrance is an impulse category. Low price, emotional purchase, low return rate, repeat buying. Your category's return rate and price point set your ceiling — apparel and electronics live in a harsher margin world.
  • The subsidy window is closing. Platform incentives, cheap acquisition, and low competition were 2024–2025 conditions. Entering today means a different, harder game.
  • TikTok-shared figures aren't audited. The $32M figure comes from the founder via a TikTok-supplied data story; Modern Retail notes the underlying platform data was shared by TikTok itself. Directionally real, precisely unverified.
