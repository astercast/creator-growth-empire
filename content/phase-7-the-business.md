# Phase 7 — The Business

> You're not a YouTuber with income. You're a company with a YouTube division. This phase makes it legal, clean, and real.

**Not legal or tax advice.** This phase is educational. Business structures, tax obligations, and costs vary wildly by state and country. Talk to a licensed CPA and/or attorney before forming anything. What follows is the map, not the territory.

## Why formalize at all

Three reasons, in order of importance:

1. **Liability separation** — if the business gets sued (copyright claim, sponsor dispute, product issue), your personal assets aren't on the table. An LLC is a legal wall between "you" and "the company."
2. **Tax clarity** — business income and expenses in one clean bucket. Deductible expenses (equipment, software, home office, contractors) only work if the money is separated.
3. **Credibility** — brands, platforms, and banks take "LLC Inc." more seriously than a personal PayPal. Sponsors over a certain size *require* contracting with a business entity.

**When:** when monthly profit consistently covers the formation + maintenance costs (often $50–$500/year depending on state, plus registered agent fees). For most creators that's somewhere in the $1k–$3k/month profit band. Before that, a sole proprietorship with clean books is fine.

## LLC formation: the practical walkthrough

The typical US path (other countries: research your local equivalent — Ltd, GmbH, etc.):

1. **Choose your state** — usually your home state. (Delaware/Wyoming/Nevada get hyped online; for a small creator business, home state is almost always simpler and cheaper.)
2. **Pick a name** — check availability with your Secretary of State. Consider a *holding* name ("[Name] Media LLC") rather than your channel name, so it covers future channels and products.
3. **File Articles of Organization** — usually online, $50–$500 depending on state.
4. **Registered agent** — a legal requirement; ~$100–$300/year through a service, or yourself if you have a stable address.
5. **Operating agreement** — even for a single member. Templates exist; it defines ownership and operations.
6. **EIN** — free from the IRS website. Your business's Social Security number; needed for bank accounts and tax filings.
7. **Business bank account** — open it the week the LLC is approved. This is the wall. All business money flows here.

**S-corp election (later):** once profit is consistently high (often cited around $60k+/year net, varies), electing S-corp taxation can reduce self-employment tax. This is CPA territory — the savings are real but the paperwork is not DIY.

## Taxes: the system that prevents disasters

The #1 financial killer of creators isn't low income — it's the **surprise tax bill** in April on money already spent.

**The non-negotiable system:**
- **Separate everything** — business bank account + business credit/debit card. Zero personal spending from business accounts. Ever.
- **Set aside 25–30%** of every payment *the day it arrives*, into a tax savings account. Quarterly estimated taxes are the law for the self-employed in the US; penalties for skipping are real.
- **Track every expense** — equipment, software, subscriptions, home office %, internet %, travel for content, contractors. Every untracked expense is money you overpay in tax.
- **Quarterly ritual** — 2 hours, 4 times a year: reconcile books, pay estimates, review profit. Put it on the calendar like an upload.

**Common creator deductions** (US, verify with your CPA): camera/audio/lighting gear, editing software, music licensing, home office (simplified or actual), internet/phone percentage, travel *primarily* for content, contractors and editors, merch production costs, education (courses, books in your niche).

## Bookkeeping: boring, load-bearing

You don't need to love accounting. You need a system:

- **Month 1–12 of profit:** a spreadsheet. Income tab, expense tab, monthly totals. Free and sufficient.
- **When it gets complex** (multiple income streams, inventory, contractors): bookkeeping software (the usual suspects all work; pick one and commit).
- **The monthly close:** last day of the month, 30 minutes — categorize everything, check the tax set-aside, note anything weird. Twelve 30-minute sessions beat one panicked week in April.

## Hiring your first help: buy back your time

The first hire isn't a luxury — it's the highest-ROI move once editing (or admin) eats 10+ hours/week. The math: if your hour creating is worth $100+ (sponsor math says it is) and an editor costs $25–$50/hour, every hour you edit instead of create *loses* money.

**Hire in this order:** editor → thumbnail designer → virtual assistant (inbox, scheduling, comments) → channel manager. One role at a time, with SOPs (Phase 8) before each hire.

**How:** start with contractors (Upwork, niche communities, your own audience — fans make fiercely loyal hires). Paid trial project first, always. Clear deliverables, clear pay, clear deadlines. Classify correctly (contractor vs. employee is a legal distinction with tax consequences — CPA question, not a guess).

## Contracts: the 3 you need first

1. **Sponsor agreement** — scope, deliverables, timeline, payment terms (50% upfront), usage rights, kill fee. Never work on a handshake above $500.
2. **Contractor agreement** — work-for-hire language (you own the output), payment, deadlines, confidentiality.
3. **Collab agreement** — revenue splits and content rights *in writing* before filming. Friendships have died over unwritten splits.

## Hiring: the trial project (do this exactly)

**The job post (one paragraph):** what you need, the style (link 3 examples), pay per project, and "paid trial: [specific small task], $[X]." Post in niche communities and to your audience — fans make loyal hires, but *always* run the trial.

**The trial:** a real, small, paid piece of work — one edited short, 5 thumbnails, one week of inbox management. Pay fairly even if you reject them; word travels.

**Evaluate on:** output quality (70%), communication speed (20%), and how they handle one round of feedback (10%). Someone great at the work but terrible at feedback will cost you more than their salary in friction.

**Onboarding:** their first week is your SOPs (Phase 8) + two supervised projects. By week two they should ship without you. If they can't, the SOPs — not the person — are usually the problem.

## Sole prop vs. LLC: the decision framework

| | Sole proprietorship | LLC |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | $0 | $50–$500 + $100–$300/yr |
| Liability wall | None | Yes |
| Taxes | Pass-through, self-employment tax | Pass-through (default); S-corp election possible later |
| Admin | Nothing | Annual filings, separate books |
| Right when | Profit under ~$1k/mo | Profit consistently over ~$1k–$3k/mo |

Neither is "better" — they're stages. Start as a sole prop with clean books; formalize when the profit justifies the overhead. The mistake isn't choosing wrong — it's choosing *nothing* and commingling money for years.

## The quarterly tax calendar (US)

- **Jan 15** — Q4 estimated payment (prior year)
- **Apr 15** — Q1 estimate + annual filing
- **Jun 15** — Q2 estimate
- **Sep 15** — Q3 estimate

Miss a quarter and the penalty clock starts. Calendar invites, recurring, with a 1-week warning. This is a 2-hour task that prevents 5-figure disasters.

## Insurance: the boring shield

Once you have real revenue: **general liability** (if you film in public/commercial spaces or sell products) and **equipment coverage** (a $5k camera kit is worth a $200/year rider). Umbrella policies get cheap above certain asset levels. Unsexy, essential — one lawsuit or one stolen gear bag without coverage ends businesses.

## Business credit: build it before you need it

Once the LLC and bank account exist, get a business credit card — even with a small limit. Use it for all business expenses, pay in full monthly. You're building a business credit profile that later unlocks real limits for inventory runs, equipment, and ads — without touching personal credit. Never carry a balance to "build credit"; on-time full payments build it faster and cost nothing. Keep personal guarantees in mind: most small-business cards still hold you personally liable, which is another reason the liability wall matters.

## Questions you're asking

**"Do I need a CPA or can I DIY with software?"** DIY until ~$50k/year profit, then get a CPA for at least an annual review. The software handles arithmetic; the CPA handles *strategy* (entity choice, S-corp timing, deduction optimization). One good CPA meeting routinely pays for itself several times over.

**"What if I already commingled money for a year?"** Fix it going forward *today*, then reconstruct: pull 12 months of statements, categorize everything, and hand it to a bookkeeper for a cleanup (usually a few hundred dollars). The IRS cares far more about honest reconstruction than perfect history.

**"Employee vs. contractor — does it matter yet?"** Yes, from hire one. Misclassification penalties are brutal and retroactive. The short version: if you control *how and when* they work, they're likely an employee in most jurisdictions. When in doubt, this is a 30-minute CPA/attorney question, not a guess.

**"Should the business own my channel?"** Structure the IP sensibly: the LLC should own the channel assets, brand, and content library — that's what makes the company sellable and protected. Your personal employment/contractor relationship with your own LLC is another CPA conversation, but the principle stands: the *business* owns the *assets*.
