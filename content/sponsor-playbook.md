**The honesty banner:** this playbook teaches you to price your work, not to guarantee you'll sell it. A ~5% cold-pitch reply rate is normal. Rates change constantly — verify against current creator rate reports before you quote.

## 1. The five deal structures — what brands actually buy

A sponsorship is not one product. It's five different transactions, and confusing them is how creators leave the most money on the table. Every brand deal is some combination of these — the Deal Builder tool prices them as separate line items, because that's how the money actually moves.

**Flat fee (per post/video).** The brand pays a fixed price for the deliverable: a dedicated video, an integrated slot, a short-form post. This is the default structure for nano through macro creators and the only structure where the 2026 rate card gives you tier ranges to quote from. The negotiation happens on the number, not the structure. Simple, predictable, and — for creators under ~500K followers — almost always the structure you want, because the brand absorbs the performance risk.

**CPM / performance-linked.** The brand pays per thousand verified views (a CPM deal), or pays a base fee plus a bonus at view thresholds. Sponsored CPMs in 2026: YouTube integrated slot $20–$35, dedicated YouTube video $50–$90, TikTok/YouTube general $10–$30, Instagram sponsored Reel median $22.40 (all creator-reported, [verified] across agency compilations). The trap: CPM deals transfer performance risk to you. If the video underperforms your average, you earn less for identical work. Take CPM only when the implied CPM beats your flat-fee math *and* you have the view history to de-risk it — otherwise, flat fee.

**Affiliate / commission.** The brand pays a percentage of sales through your link or code — TikTok Shop's affiliate commissions run ~5–20% by category (electronics ~5%, beauty/wellness up to ~30%, targeted rates up to 50% [single-source]). The math is honest when you have buying-intent content (reviews, comparisons, gift guides) and brutal when you don't. Pure affiliate with no flat fee is a risk-transfer to the creator: no guaranteed payment for guaranteed work. Rule of thumb that has held across every wave of this guide's research: affiliate-only deals are for creators whose content already converts — for everyone else, affiliate is the *bonus* on top of a flat fee, never the whole deal.

**Whitelisting.** The brand runs paid ads *from your account* — your handle, your face, their ad budget behind it. This is a separate product from the post itself, and it has separate pricing: 30–100% of the base fee **per month** (projectcasting 2026, [single-source] — the same benchmark the rate card carries). Three rules: it's monthly, it's revocable (you can pull the plug — write that in), and it's never perpetual. A brand running your face as their ad creative for six months is buying six months of your credibility; price it like that.

**Usage rights (paid-ad licensing).** The brand re-runs your content as paid ads from *their* account. The most-neglected line item in creator deals, and the most expensive one to get wrong: when a brand runs your $300 video as their paid ad for a year, they've captured thousands in creative value for the price of one post. Section 3 has the full 2026 pricing grid — the rule that matters here is architectural: usage is always its own line item, never folded into the post fee.

**How the five combine:** a typical mid-size deal is flat fee + usage add-on + 30-day whitelisting. The post fee is the base; everything else stacks. If a brand offers one number for "the post plus usage plus whitelisting," that's not a package — it's a discount they're hoping you won't unbundle.

## 2. The tier table, honestly: nano → mega as reported ranges

The full tier table lives on the [2026 Rate Card](rate-card.html) — per-video/per-post ranges for nano (1K–10K), micro (10K–100K), macro (100K–1M), and mega (1M+) across YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram, plus the platform multipliers (YouTube ~1.7× Instagram, TikTok ~0.85× at equal followers). This section is what the table *doesn't* tell you: how wide the honest disagreement is, and what fresh 2026 surveys add.

**The benchmarks disagree 2–3× within tiers — say it out loud.** Click Analytic's 2026 crawl has nano at $114–$679 per post where other benchmarks read $25–$300; mega reads $12,200+ against $20K–$100K+ elsewhere ([single-source] each). This isn't a data error — it's the market. Treat the low end as "small brand, simple deliverable, weak engagement proof" and the high end as "proven engagement, competitive niche, paid-ad usage priced in." When someone quotes you one number, ask which end of the disagreement they're reading from.

**Fresh 2026 datapoints that sit around the table:**

- **Quoted medians from 2,356 rate-card lines (Sep 2026, [single-source]):** Instagram Reel $1,800, TikTok video $2,000, video integration $3,000, UGC without posting $500. These are medians of what creators *list* — remember the 23% rule below.
- **TikTok-specific tier table (Influencerfee, Oct 2026, [single-source]):** nano $25–$150 per in-feed video up to mega $20,000–$100K — with the note that TikTok's reach advantage prices it roughly **30–50% below Instagram** at equivalent follower counts. Directionally consistent with the 0.85× multiplier the rate card carries; keep the discrepancy labeled.
- **UGC without posting (Storika, Oct 2026, [single-source]):** beginner $150–$400 per 30-second TikTok-style video, intermediate $400–$1,000. UGC is its own market — the brand buys the creative, not the distribution. Price it off production effort, not your follower count.
- **Video commands a 20–40% premium** over static formats; **multi-platform bundles add 30–50%** — selling the same concept across YouTube + TikTok + Instagram is more than the sum of the parts (rate-card negotiation data, [single-source]).

**The 23% rule (projectcasting / InfluenceFlow 2026, 1,347 creator profiles, re-verified Oct 2026):** creators list rates ~23% above what they actually accept (the nano gap is biggest, ~28%). This is why the playbook says to quote at the top of your tier range: the market expects to negotiate you down, and the data says the landing zone is roughly 23% below the open. A $10,000 open lands around $7,700. Price the negotiation into the number.

**Per-follower rates fall as accounts grow** — nano runs ~$25–$50 per 1K followers, macro ~$10–$12 per 1K. Follower count is the weakest pricing variable. Engagement rate and expected views are what you're actually selling; the tier table is the starting grid, not the finish line. Verify against current creator rate reports before you quote — rates change, and a 2026 number quoted in 2027 is a guess.

## 3. Usage rights: the most-neglected line item

This section exists because this is where creators lose the most money per deal. The sentence that matters: **when a brand re-runs your $300 video as paid ads for a year, they're capturing thousands in value.** A $300 video run as a paid ad for a year is a multi-thousand-dollar asset — it replaces creative the brand would otherwise pay an agency five figures to produce. Price it like an asset, not a favor.

**The 2026 usage-rights grid** (projectcasting 2026, [single-source], re-verified Oct 2026 — the same figures the rate card and Deal Analyzer carry; Storika's Oct 2026 UGC figures corroborate the shape):

| Usage term | Add-on, % of base post fee | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Organic only | +0% | Base post fee covers organic placement on your channel only. The absence of a line item — not a market rate. |
| Paid ads, 3 months | +25–50% | Brand re-runs your content as paid ads for 3 months, from their account. |
| Paid ads, 3–6 months | +50–75% | Six months of someone else's media budget behind your face. |
| Paid ads, 6–12 months | +75–100% | Cap it in writing — a hard end date, or it becomes the next row. |
| Perpetual / unlimited | From +100% (2× base), open-ended | A perpetual paid-ads license turns your post into the brand's permanent ad creative. Price as its own line item — or refuse. |

Storika's corroborating shape ([single-source], Oct 2026): +25–40% for expanded paid use, +50–100% for unlimited perpetual rights, +20–50% for paid-ad usage. Same staircase, same lesson.

**The architecture is the lesson, not the percentages.** Add-ons are percentages *on top of* the base post fee, priced as *separate line items* — never folded into the post fee. When a brand says "our budget is $X all-in," the correct response unbundles: $A for the post, $B for 3 months of paid-ad usage, $C for whitelisting. One bundled number hides which part you're discounting.

**The math that makes it click.** Take a micro creator whose dedicated video benchmarks at $4,000 (mid-range of the $1K–$10K micro/YouTube band). Paid-ad usage for 12 months adds +75–100% — $3,000–$4,000 — as its own line. Whitelisting for 2 months at 30–100%/month adds another $2,400–$8,000. The post was $4,000; the *deal* is $9,400–$16,000. Every line you let the brand fold into the post fee is a line you're giving away. This is a worked illustration of the benchmark mechanics, not a quote — your tier range is yours.

**Perpetual usage deserves its own paragraph.** "Perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, in all media now known or hereafter devised" is boilerplate that turns your post into the brand's permanent ad creative for the price of one post. Two honest options: price it from 2× the base fee as its own line item, or refuse outright. There is no third option where you sign it at organic price and feel fine later. If the brand insists, the negotiation script in the [Deal Room](deal-room.html) and the usage-rights upsell template in [deal-templates.json](data/v1/deal-templates.json) carry the exact language.

**Why brands ask to fold usage into the post fee:** because it works. "We don't do usage fees, it's just part of the partnership" is a negotiation position, not a market fact. The benchmark grid exists precisely so you can name the tier, name the multiplier, and name the end date. Specificity is what turns a hope into a line item.

## 4. Whitelisting: ads from *your* account, priced monthly

Whitelisting confuses creators because it *looks* like posting. It isn't. Whitelisting = the brand runs paid ads through your handle — your profile picture, your name, their targeting and budget. Your audience sees an ad wearing your face. That costs more than a post, for three reasons: it's your credibility being spent, it's metered by the month (the longer it runs, the more of your trust it burns), and you can revoke it — which means the brand is renting, not buying.

**Pricing:** 30–100% of the base fee **per month** (projectcasting 2026, [single-source]). The range is wide because the value is wide: 30 days of whitelisting on a product launch is a different asset than 6 months of always-on performance creative. Price the term, not the vibe.

**The three whitelisting rules:**

1. **Monthly, always.** A "one-time whitelisting fee" with no term is perpetual whitelisting wearing a costume. Every whitelisting line item carries a month count.
2. **Revocable, in writing.** You can pull the plug — brand misbehaves, product turns out bad, your audience revolts. The revocation clause is the difference between renting your credibility and selling it.
3. **Never perpetual.** Perpetual whitelisting at any price is selling your face to a media buyer forever. Refuse, or price it as a separate negotiation where the number reflects what "forever" actually costs (it doesn't — no number does, which is the point).

**Whitelisting vs. usage rights:** usage = the brand runs your content as ads from *their* account (priced by term, Section 3). Whitelisting = ads run from *your* account (priced monthly). A deal can carry both — the post, the usage license, and the whitelisting window are three separate line items. When a contract says "whitelisting included," ask: included at what monthly rate, for how many months, revocable how?

## 5. Negotiation mechanics: anchoring, bundles, timing, leverage

Negotiation isn't charisma — it's arithmetic with leverage. The mechanics below are the ones with 2026 data behind them.

**Anchor high, land in the zone.** Creators list ~23% above what they accept (Section 2). Quote at the top of your tier range and expect to be negotiated down into the ~23% landing zone. A $10,000 open lands around $7,700 — that isn't losing the negotiation, that's the market working. The mistake is opening *at* $7,700 and landing at $5,900.

**Raise 20% every 3 closed deals until someone says no.** From the sponsorship-pitch procedure: your price is a discovery process. Close three deals at your current rate, raise 20%, keep going — when your close rate drops to ~1 in 3 conversations, you've found your price. This is the only honest way to price something the market won't tell you directly.

**Bundle math: 41% offer package discounts on 3+ posts.** Re-verified Oct 2026 via projectcasting (InfluenceFlow 2026). Build the discount into your bundles — don't let the brand discover it. A 3-video package at 15% off locks in revenue and saves you two more outreach cycles. The Deal Builder prices multi-deliverable packages with the bundle note attached, because the discount is a *feature* of your offer, not a concession you make under pressure.

**Exclusivity pricing (the levers that exist):** 30-day named-competitor exclusivity ≈ +25% of the base fee; 60–90 days ≈ +50% (deal-negotiation skill, 2026 benchmarks). Broad category locks for 6+ months don't have a benchmark multiplier — because the honest price is "whatever covers the deals you'd lose," which is a number only you can compute. Section 7 goes deep on the clause itself.

**Niche leverage is real and measured.** Finance and tech sponsors pay 2–3× the rule-of-thumb CPM; entertainment pays at the bottom of the band. The Deal Analyzer's niche multipliers (finance/tech 2.5×, gaming/comedy/music 0.6×) are the site's honest version of this — quote inside your niche's reality, not the market's average.

**Timing: Q4 is 29–31% of the year's collab volume.** The Brand Deals Report 2026 (2,341 Instagram / 3,156 TikTok / 2,360 YouTube creators measured, reported Oct 9 2026, [single-source]) puts nearly a third of annual collaborations in Q4. Pitch in August–September — Q4 budgets commit early. Two caveats that travel with the timing: Black Friday per-post rates can run *lower* than normal because brands buy in bulk, and affiliate commissions are often cut during BFCM week because brand margins are already thin (Lilach Bullock, Oct 2026, [single-source]).

**Payment speed is now a feature.** TikTok One — the platform's brand-deal marketplace — pays creators in under 30 days with zero platform cut ([verified] via Tubefilter, netinfluencer, communicateonline.me, Oct 2026). The invite-only One Plus tier pays 15% more on Custom Collab deals at no added cost to brands (100K+ followers, US/Mexico). No creator-reported payout data exists yet as of Oct 11 — announcement plus syndication only. The lesson for your own contracts: payment terms are negotiable infrastructure. Net-30 with 50% upfront from any brand you haven't worked with is the standard; net-90 without escrow means you're financing the brand's marketing for a quarter (that's a red flag, Section 8).

**The walk-away rate: decide it before the call, not during it.** From the Phase 5 negotiation procedure: your walk-away number is set when you're calm, written down, and non-negotiable in the moment. Negotiating without one is just hoping. The Deal Builder's checklist ends with this line because every other line is arithmetic and this one is discipline.

## 6. Exclusivity: the clause that can freeze your income

Exclusivity is the clause creators underestimate most. It doesn't cost you the deal in front of you — it costs you the *next three*.

**Named vs. category.** Named-competitor exclusivity ("you won't work with Brand X, Y, Z for 60 days") is a bounded, priceable thing: +25% for 30 days, +50% for 60–90 days ([single-source] — deal-negotiation benchmarks, 2026). Category exclusivity ("no competing products") is unbounded — "competing" is whatever the brand's lawyer says it is, and it can freeze out an entire vertical. The rule: narrow every exclusivity to *named competitors, in writing, with a term*. If the brand needs the full category, the premium has to cover the deals you'd actually lose — compute that number from your pipeline, not from vibes.

**The duration math.** A 30-day exclusivity on a single deliverable is a rounding error on your year. A 6-month category lock is a quarter of your sponsor income gone. A 12-month lock signed in January decides your whole year. Exclusivity longer than 90 days without a premium that covers lost deals is the single most expensive signature in this file — more expensive than a bad rate, because a bad rate ends when the video ships.

**The consistency check the Deal Builder runs:** your exclusivity window and your usage window have to agree. If the brand gets 12 months of paid-ad usage but your exclusivity ends at 90 days, you can take a competitor's money while their ads still run with your face — or worse, the reverse: your face advertises Brand A while you're contractually promoting Brand B. Flag the mismatch before you sign, not after.

**Overlap is your problem to check.** No tool can see your other contracts. If you're under an existing exclusivity and sign a second one in the same category, you've breached one of them. The checklist asks the question; only you know the answer.

## 7. Red flags — the ten traps, explained with the math

The contract red-flag checklist lives in the [Outreach Center](outreach.html) and as machine-readable data in [deal-templates.json](data/v1/deal-templates.json) — ten flags, scored (any walk-away flag, or 3+ points, means don't sign as written). The flag data lives in deal-templates.json [single-source]. This section is the *why* behind each flag — the math and the mechanism that make them expensive.

1. **Perpetual usage for organic price.** The brand's ad creative, forever, for the price of one post. The fix is arithmetic: price perpetual from 2× base as its own line item, or refuse. (Section 3.)
2. **Net-90+ payment with no escrow.** You're financing their marketing for a quarter with zero leverage if they ghost. Counter: net-30, 50% upfront from any brand you haven't worked with.
3. **Unlimited revisions.** An open revision clause is a blank check on your time — "just one more tweak" becomes a second unpaid production cycle. Cap it in writing: two rounds included, paid rate per additional round.
4. **No kill fee.** If they can cancel after you've filmed and owe you nothing, all the production risk sits on you. The kill fee is the price of your sunk time — 50% if cancelled after filming is the standard shape.
5. **Vague "all media" usage grants.** "All media now known or hereafter devised" without a term is perpetual usage wearing legalese. Every usage grant needs a named term and a named medium.
6. **Auto-renewing exclusivity.** An exclusivity that renews unless you opt out is a lock you have to remember to escape. Exclusivity terms end on a date; they don't roll over.
7. **View-guarantee clawbacks.** "If the video doesn't hit X views, you refund Y%." Your last-10 average is the honest basis for expectations — a clawback turns normal variance into your financial liability. Performance risk belongs to the brand (that's what the flat fee is for).
8. **One-sided morality clauses.** They can terminate if you embarrass them, but there's no reciprocal clause if the brand does something your audience would hate. Morality clauses cut both ways or they don't exist.
9. **Missing FTC disclosure language.** If the contract doesn't require disclosure — or worse, implies you should hide it — the brand is asking you to break the law for them. Section 10 has the 2026 rules; the fines are per violation, and they're yours.
10. **Scope creep language.** "And other promotional activities as reasonably requested" is an unlimited deliverable hiding in the boilerplate. Every deliverable is named, counted, and dated — anything else is a second deal you haven't priced.

**The scoring rule, plainly:** any single walk-away flag — or three lesser flags together — means don't sign as written. Not "don't sign ever" — *as written*. Every flag has a fix, and the fix is a counter, not a concession.

## 8. Contract basics: money, rights, and the signature

This section is educational, not legal advice — the disclaimer at the bottom is load-bearing. Contracts above $500 deserve a real lawyer's eyes; what follows is the structure a good contract has, so you can tell when yours doesn't.

**Payment terms.** Net-30 is the standard; 50% upfront from any brand you haven't worked with is the rule ([single-source] — the site's deal-negotiation procedure). Milestone splits (50/50, or 30/40/30 on larger deals) de-risk both sides. Net-60 is a negotiation; net-90 without escrow is a red flag (Section 7). Late-payment terms — what happens at day 31, day 61 — belong in the contract, not in your hopes.

**Revisions and approvals.** Two revision rounds included; additional rounds at a named rate. Approvals are *talking points, not scripts* — the brand approves factual accuracy and brand safety, not your personality. An approval window (48–72 hours) with "silence = approval" keeps one slow legal department from holding your content calendar hostage.

**Kill fees and cancellation.** If the brand cancels after you've filmed, you get paid — the standard shape is 50% of the fee. If *you* cancel, the brand's deposit terms apply. Cancellation without a kill fee means the brand holds a free option on your time.

**Exclusivity, usage, whitelisting.** Named competitors, dated terms, separate line items — Sections 3, 4, and 6 are the pricing; the contract is where the pricing becomes binding. If a term was agreed on the call but isn't in the paper, it doesn't exist.

**What a lawyer is actually for.** Not for reading the contract — this file just taught you to do that. A lawyer is for the $5,000+ deal where the brand's paper has an indemnification clause, a jurisdiction clause, or an IP assignment buried in section 11. IP assignment (the brand *owns* your content outright, not just licenses it) is the one clause in this file's orbit that changes what you made into what they own — never sign it inside a sponsorship, ever.

**Not legal advice.** This section describes common contract structures as reported across 2026 creator-economy coverage. It is not legal advice, it doesn't create an attorney-client relationship, and it can't tell you whether *your* contract is enforceable in *your* jurisdiction. For anything above $500 — or anything with an indemnification, IP assignment, or jurisdiction clause — get a real lawyer's eyes on the paper. The checklist in the [Outreach Center](outreach.html) scores structure; a lawyer judges enforceability.

## 9. FTC disclosure in 2026: the rules, the fines, the reality

The disclosure rules got sharper in 2026 — and for the first time, enforcement is aimed at individual creators at scale, not just brands.

**The rules in plain English.** Disclose the material connection — clearly, conspicuously, and *early*. In practice: #ad or equivalent in the first lines of the description *and* verbal + on-screen disclosure in the video itself. The platform's branded-content tag alone does not satisfy "clear and conspicuous" ([verified] via amerilawpc.com, elev8or.io). AI-generated endorsements need their own disclosure on top of the paid one — the "double disclosure" (2026 enforcement priority). The Endorsement Guides aren't statutes but are enforced as such; the fines below are per violation.

**The fines, correctly attributed.** Two figures circulate and both are right — for different rules. The maximum civil penalty under the FTC Act (15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(A)) is **$53,088 per violation** ([verified] via recordinglaw.com, Oct 2026 — set January 2025, held for 2026). The **$51,744** figure applies specifically under the 2024 Trade Regulation Rule on consumer reviews and testimonials ([verified] via everything-pr.com). Cite the right number for the right rule; the guide doesn't blend them.

**2026 enforcement posture.** The FTC is targeting individual creators at scale for the first time (enforcement up ~40% in 2025). October 2026 actions so far are non-creator (GOAT, Cleo AI, Southern Glazer's) — posture is guidance plus platform enforcement, but the direction is one-way. Named 2026 priorities: AI-generated endorsements, deepfakes, podcast ads, affiliate links, and child-directed content (kurums.com, Oct 2026, [single-source]). The FTC also opened an Impersonation Rule rulemaking (Oct 1, 2026; comments close **November 30, 2026**) asking whether the rule should reach platform ad tools — worth watching, not actionable yet. Not legal advice.

**The disclosure reality: the numbers are bad.** The Brand Deals Report 2026 (reported Oct 9, 2026; 2,341 Instagram / 3,156 TikTok / 2,360 YouTube creators measured, [single-source]) found disclosure rates of **TikTok 52%, YouTube 42%, Instagram 29%** — 61% of Instagram sponsored posts unlabeled. An academic study (SAGE Journals, 2026; classifier over 100M+ tweets) found **>96% of sponsored posts undisclosed** — that's Twitter vs. video platforms with a different methodology, so present both labeled and don't reconcile them. The honest read: non-disclosure is the norm, and the norm is now the enforcement target.

**The counterintuitive good news: disclosure doesn't cost you engagement.** The InfluenceFlow 2026 audit (1,449 creator profiles, [single-source] vendor data) found 78% of profiles include required disclosures — #ad/#sponsored in 82% of compliant posts vs. native platform tools in only 54% — and disclosed sponsored content posted median engagement of **10.4% on TikTok and 3.9% on Instagram**. Transparency doesn't penalize engagement. The audience doesn't punish honesty; the algorithm doesn't either. The only party disclosure hurts is the brand that wanted it hidden — which is exactly why the contract must require it (red flag 9).

**The tax footnote.** The 2026 1099 threshold moved from $600 to $2,000 (One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 2025; payments on/after Jan 1, 2026) — but income below the threshold is still taxable, and the IRS now runs automated cross-platform income matching. Keep the sponsor ledger the [Deal Room](deal-room.html) exports; your accountant will want it. Not tax advice.

## 10. The budget tailwind: why brands are spending more

Sponsorship pricing isn't just your negotiation — it's the market behind it. The 2026 market is unusually friendly to creators, and the data says why.

**Budgets are expanding.** The Kolsquare survey (Sept 2026; 1,123 European marketing decision-makers, [single-source]) found **93% of UK organisations plan to expand influencer spending** — up from 81% last year and 50% two years ago. 31% expect to increase influencer partnerships by **50% or more**. Short-form video is the primary opportunity for 55% of respondents. This is a demand curve moving in your favor.

**The money is migrating downmarket.** The Influencer Marketing Hub's 2026 read: brand budgets keep migrating toward nano and micro creators — smaller creators are the value buy (macro costs ~45% more per authentic engagement), and brands know it. If you're under 100K followers, the market isn't tolerating you — it's hunting you.

**Long partnerships beat one-offs.** The Brand Deals Report 2026 measured average partnership length at **13.5 months on YouTube vs. 4.9 on TikTok** ([single-source]). The playbook implication: your best negotiation isn't the rate on video one — it's the retainer conversation after video three performed. Brands pay for proven working relationships; the first deal is the audition.

**Creators are the affiliate channel now.** Adobe's 2026 holiday forecast: creators drive **22% of affiliate traffic**, with affiliate/partner share of retail revenue up 12% and social media's share up 17%. Sponsorship and affiliate aren't separate games anymore — the same audience trust converts in both.

## 11. The build order: outbound → quote → verify → sign

The tools on this site form a pipeline. In order:

1. **Target.** Build the 20-brand list from the sponsorship-pitch procedure — brands sponsoring channels one size above yours first, then their competitors. Find the human; never "dear partnerships team."
2. **Pitch.** The [Outreach Center](outreach.html) runs the outbound system: the pipeline tracker, the honest pitch templates (under 120 words, every stat a bracket you fill), the one 7-day nudge. ~5% reply rate is normal ([single-source] — the sponsorship-pitch procedure); the system works on volume.
3. **Build the quote.** The [Deal Builder](deal-builder.html) assembles your package: deliverables in, line-item quote out — base fees from the 2026 rate card, usage and whitelisting as separate lines, exclusivity priced or flagged, a ready-to-send proposal, and the deal checklist.
4. **Verify the offer.** When the brand replies with terms, the [Deal Analyzer](deal-analyzer.html) benchmarks the offer against the rate card with niche adjustment, scans the terms, applies the contract checklist, and builds the counter-offer worksheet.
5. **Manage to paid.** The [Deal Room](deal-room.html) carries the deal from offer to paid — the ledger your accountant wants, the usage pricing, the negotiation scripts.

**When to walk away.** No flat fee (affiliate-only with no track record to justify it). Perpetual usage at organic price. Broad exclusivity without a premium. Net-90 without escrow. A contract that hides disclosure. Walking away from a bad deal isn't losing a sponsor — it's keeping the calendar slot for a good one.

**The honesty rule for your audience.** Vet every product before you pitch it — your audience blames *you*, not the brand, when the product is bad. Disclose every paid relationship, clearly and early (Section 9). The trust that makes sponsors pay you is the same trust a bad sponsorship destroys. Price accordingly.
